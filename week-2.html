The building blocks of project controls — Scope, Time, and Cost

BE
Barış Emre Özçelik, PMP Planning & Project Controls Lead · Jun 27, 2026
9 min read

Video coming soon

Before we open Primavera...

Last week, we learned an important lesson. Projects don't fail because of software. They fail because reality was never modeled correctly.

But that raises a new question. What exactly are we trying to model?

Before we ever build a schedule. Before we define activities. Before we calculate the Critical Path. We first need to understand the three forces that control every construction project.

Scope. Time. Cost.

Everything else is built on top of them.

Imagine building a house

Imagine someone asks you to build a house.

The first question isn't "How long will it take?" It isn't "How much will it cost?"

The first question is much simpler: "What exactly are we building?"

Three bedrooms? Five floors? A reinforced concrete frame? Luxury finishes? A swimming pool?

Without defining the scope, neither time nor cost can be estimated with confidence.

Everything begins with scope.

The three building blocks

Every successful project balances three elements.

Scope defines what will be delivered. Time defines when it will be delivered. Cost defines how much it will take to deliver it.

None of these can exist independently. Change one — the other two immediately react.

That's why Project Controls isn't about managing schedules. It's about managing relationships.

The rule every planner must accept

Many projects begin with a dangerous conversation. The client says:

"I need the same scope. I have the same budget. I just want it finished three months earlier."

Sounds reasonable. Unfortunately, construction doesn't work like that.

If the deadline becomes shorter, something else must change. Either more resources, more budget, or less scope.

There is no fourth option.

This isn't an opinion. It's mathematics.

Whenever someone changes one corner, the triangle immediately becomes unstable. A professional planner doesn't simply accept impossible targets. A professional planner explains their consequences.

The nervous system of every project

If Scope, Time, and Cost form the body, the Work Breakdown Structure is the nervous system.

Everything depends on it. Progress. Costs. Forecasts. Reports. Dashboards. Earned Value.

Everything starts with the WBS.

A simple example

Imagine your project consists of two buildings. A deliverable-based WBS is simple: Building A, Building B.

Now imagine the client's budget is suddenly reduced by 40%. You can still complete Building A. The client receives a finished asset.

Now imagine your WBS looks different — organized by phase instead: Design, Procurement, Construction, Commissioning.

The budget is reduced halfway. Now every phase stops halfway. Nothing is finished. Nothing can be handed over.

The project has consumed money — without creating value.

That's why experienced planners organize work around deliverables — not project phases.

Four components that turn planning into a model

Once the scope has been structured correctly, the schedule itself is surprisingly simple. Every schedule is built from only four components.

Activities represent work. Milestones represent important events. Resources perform the work. Logic connects everything together.

Individually, they're just data. Together, they become a forecasting model.

Why these rules exist

Many people think these rules were created by software companies. They weren't.

They were created because governments and project owners lost enormous amounts of money. Large defence programs. Infrastructure projects. Public investments. All suffered from the same problem: poor visibility.

Modern Project Controls evolved because organizations needed reliable information before making expensive decisions. That's why these principles still matter today.

The big picture

Week One taught us: a schedule is a model.

Week Two teaches us: that model stands on Scope, Time, and Cost.

Without balance, no software can rescue the project.

Key takeaways

Before moving to Week Three, remember these ideas:

✔ Every project is governed by Scope, Time, and Cost.
✔ Changing one always affects the other two.
✔ Scope must be structured through a deliverable-based WBS.
✔ Activities, Milestones, Resources, and Logic create the schedule model.
✔ Project Controls is about balancing systems — not updating software.
